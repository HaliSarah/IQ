Predicting Economic Damage from U.S. Storm Events

Radar can tell you a storm is coming. It can't tell you what it will cost. This project tests whether the characteristics NOAA already records about a storm event — before its full impact is known — carry enough signal to predict economic damage on their own.

  • 410,311 storm events
  • 0.880 ROC-AUC, Random Forest
  • NOAA data, 2020–2025
Read the full report (PDF) Watch the presentation Code & notebooks

Business Problem

Two kinds of uncertainty

Weather forecasting is rooted in using data to reduce the uncertainty of our climate — radar, satellites and predictive models let us quantify and visualize storms as they happen. But radar can't measure what an event will do to people, property and communities. That's a second, harder form of uncertainty: impact.

Economic damage from severe weather is costly and difficult to anticipate. Our team wanted to know whether the characteristics NOAA already records about a storm — before its full impact is known — carry enough signal to predict that damage on their own.

Research question: Can we predict whether a weather event will result in economic damage, based on recorded event characteristics in the NOAA Storm Events Database (2020–2025)?

Data source
NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, Storm Events Database
Study period
January 2020 – December 2025
Predictors
Event type, region, season, storm duration
Target

Where the Data Pointed

Occurrence and cost are different problems

Before modeling anything, we compared how often each storm type occurs against how often it's actually tied to recorded economic damage. The patterns weren't the same — which is exactly why this was worth modeling in the first place.

The same split shows up in total dollars: Hurricane events are far less common than something like Thunderstorm Wind, but account for the largest total losses in the dataset by a wide margin ($50.6B vs. $1.7B).

Hypothesis

What we expected going in

Event type is the strongest predictor of recorded economic damage, with region, storm duration and seasonal timing contributing secondary signal. We expected storm characteristics available near an event's onset to beat a no-skill baseline by a meaningful margin — supporting their use for forward-looking risk assessment by emergency managers, insurers and local governments.

Methods

From raw records to a modeling table

410,311storm events, 2020–2025
54→12columns cleaned to modeling features
75% / 25%known vs. unknown economic outcome
2 modelsLogistic Regression + Random Forest

Evaluation: Average Precision (PR-AUC) alongside ROC-AUC, since the positive class — events with recorded damage — is only about 23% of the data.

Train/test split: grouped by storm episode (EPISODE_ID), not by individual row. Events from the same storm system share nearly identical characteristics — region, season and duration all move together — so a row-level split would let the model see near-duplicate records across training and testing, inflating how good it looks. Grouping by episode keeps every event from one storm system on the same side of the split.

Recommendations & Ethical Considerations

Who this affects — and where it could go wrong

Stakeholder impact

Emergency managers, local governments and insurers could use these patterns to prepare and allocate resources. The risk: over-relying on results for storm types or regions that are rare or less consistently recorded.

Data assumptions & bias

The model assumes NOAA's historical records are accurate and complete. Reporting practices vary by storm type and have changed over time, and the model assumes future events follow past patterns — a limitation we state plainly wherever these results are used.

Conclusion

Back to the real question

Our interest was never just whether a storm happens — it was what it costs once it does. Heat and Excessive Heat are among the most frequent events in the dataset, yet almost none carry a recorded economic outcome, while far rarer events like Strong Wind and Tornado are disproportionately tied to damage. As the mix of severe weather shifts, planning that leans on frequency alone risks preparing for the wrong thing.

Frequency tells you what's common — not what's costly.

Next steps: layering in FEMA National Risk Index exposure and vulnerability data, so predictions reflect who and what is actually in a storm's path, not just the storm's own characteristics.
