Restated Objectives¶

Primary Objective: Build a custom Named Entity Recognition system identifying four domain-specific entity labels — not recognized by the default spaCy pipeline — across real peer-reviewed screen-time research text.

Step 2: Fetch Real Corpus from BioC-PMC API¶

The BioC-PMC API returns full article text organized into passage objects — Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion sections all included. This is the complete article body, not just the abstract. A single article from this corpus runs 8,000–20,000 words, which means even two or three articles exceed the 50,000-word requirement comfortably.

Using real full-text research literature matters for this project specifically: the entities the custom NER system targets — TikTok, adolescents, depression, sleep disruption — appear at realistic frequencies with the linguistic context they actually carry in scientific writing. That context is what makes the sentiment analysis meaningful.

BIOC_BASE = "https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/research/bionlp/RESTful/pmcoa.cgi/BioC_json/{}/unicode"

def fetch_pmc_full_text(pmc_id):
    """
    Fetches full-text passage content from the BioC-PMC API.
    Returns all passage texts joined into a single string.
    Only passages over 20 characters are included to skip
    table headers and formatting artifacts.
    """
    url = BIOC_BASE.format(pmc_id)
    req = urllib.request.Request(url, headers={'User-Agent': 'Mozilla/5.0'})
    resp = urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=30)
    data = json.loads(resp.read())
    passages = []
    for collection in data:
        for doc in collection.get('documents', []):
            for passage in doc.get('passages', []):
                t = passage.get('text', '').strip()
                if t and len(t) > 20:
                    passages.append(t)
    return ' '.join(passages)

Step 3: Build the Custom NER Pipeline¶

The EntityRuler is placed before="ner" so custom rules take priority over the statistical model. The default en_core_web_sm model was trained on OntoNotes — a general news corpus that has never seen screen-time research terminology. Without the EntityRuler in front, screen time gets labeled as nothing, adolescents becomes a generic noun with no entity tag, and depression might get labeled as a weather event.

The pattern vocabulary is expanded from the Milestone 2 baseline to cover the full range of terms that appear in real clinical research text — including variants like screen-time vs screen time, well-being vs wellbeing, and behavioural vs behavioral that appear across different article writing styles.

Step 4: Run Entity Extraction and VADER Sentiment¶

The pipeline processes each article sentence by sentence. Splitting at the sentence level before applying VADER is important: sentiment in research text operates at the sentence scale, not the document scale. A paper can discuss depression in a negative framing in one sentence and present a positive intervention finding in the next. Document-level sentiment would average these into a meaningless neutral score.

Each sentence that contains at least one custom entity gets a row in the results DataFrame — one row per entity, with the sentence-level compound score attached. This lets the analysis ask: when depression appears in a sentence, what is the sentiment of that sentence? That question is only answerable with sentence-level processing.

analyzer = SentimentIntensityAnalyzer()
CUSTOM_LABELS = {"PLATFORM", "AGE_GROUP", "HEALTH_OUTCOME", "REGULATORY_ENTITY"}

results = []

for i, (doc_text, title) in enumerate(zip(raw_docs, PMC_TITLES)):
    # Parse the full document for sentence segmentation
    spacy_doc = nlp(doc_text)
    sentences = [s.text.strip() for s in spacy_doc.sents if len(s.text.strip()) > 20]

    for sent in sentences:
        sdoc = nlp(sent)
        scores   = analyzer.polarity_scores(sent)
        compound = scores['compound']
        sentiment = (
            "positive" if compound >= 0.05
            else "negative" if compound <= -0.05
            else "neutral"
        )
        for ent in sdoc.ents:
            if ent.label_ in CUSTOM_LABELS:
                results.append({
                    'PMC_ID':    PMC_IDS[i],
                    'Article':   title[:50],
                    'Entity':    ent.text,
                    'Label':     ent.label_,
                    'Sentiment': sentiment,
                    'Compound':  round(compound, 3),
                    'Sentence':  sent[:150]
                })

    print(f"  Article {i+1} ({PMC_IDS[i]}): {len(sentences):,} sentences")

df = pd.DataFrame(results)
print(f"\nTotal entity extractions: {len(df):,}")

Formal Write-Up: Observations, Conclusions, and Ethical Assessment¶

Objectives and Achievement¶

This project pursued two objectives established in Milestone 1. The primary objective was to build a custom Named Entity Recognition system capable of identifying four domain-specific entity categories — Platform, Age Group, Health Outcome, and Regulatory Entity — in screen-time discourse. The secondary objective was to apply that system to a real multi-source corpus and determine whether entity distribution and sentiment framing differed meaningfully across the text.

Both objectives were achieved. The finalized pipeline combined spaCy's EntityRuler component with VADER sentence-level sentiment analysis, applied to 64,006 words of real peer-reviewed research fetched from PubMed Central's open-access BioC API. Across 2,540 sentences from five articles, the system produced 2,501 entity extractions spanning all four custom labels: 947 Health Outcome, 803 Age Group, 743 Platform, and 8 Regulatory Entity.

Observations¶

Scale matters. The most immediate difference between a real corpus and the 15-sentence demonstration from earlier milestones is that patterns become visible at scale. Screen time appeared 507 times across the corpus — making it the single most frequent entity. Adolescents appeared 317 times, mental health 295 times. These frequencies confirm that the entity schema is grounded in the actual language of the domain rather than imposed from outside.

Health outcomes skew strongly negative. Of the health outcome entities with at least 10 appearances, depression appeared in negative-sentiment sentences 93% of the time (122 of 131 appearances). anxiety appeared negative 80% of the time (101 of 126). depressive symptoms was negative 77% of the time. The entities that showed lower negative rates — mental health (43%), well-being (40%), academic performance (52%) — are broader terms that appear in both problem-framing and solution-framing sentences. Clinical research discusses depression as a harm; it discusses mental health as both a concern and a goal.

Screen time sentiment is balanced. The most surprising finding is that screen time — the central entity of the entire domain — appeared in negative sentences (181 times), positive sentences (188 times), and neutral sentences (138 times) at nearly equal rates. This reflects a genuine feature of the scientific literature: research papers present associations, interventions, and recommendations alongside risk findings. The text is not uniformly alarmist. A sentence reporting that limiting screen time improved sleep outcomes scores positive. A sentence reporting that excessive screen time predicted depression scores negative. The distribution across sentiment categories is the correct output — it reflects what the literature actually says.

Platform-specific entities are rare. Named platforms (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Snapchat) each appeared fewer than 10 times across 64,006 words. The peer-reviewed research literature overwhelmingly uses general terms like screen time, social media, and electronic media rather than naming specific applications. This is a meaningful finding for the NER project: a system designed to track platform-specific discourse would need to prioritize news and social media sources, where named platforms appear far more frequently than in academic abstracts and full-text research articles.

Regulatory Entity entities are sparse. Only 8 extractions across the full corpus — WHO, AAP, NIH, and screen time guidelines. This reflects the nature of the source material: peer-reviewed research cites regulatory bodies infrequently in the body text. Policy-oriented news articles would yield far denser Regulatory Entity coverage. A multi-source corpus combining research articles with news and Reddit text would produce a more balanced distribution across all four label categories.

Conclusions¶

The EntityRuler approach works well as a foundational NER layer for this domain. Its transparency — every extraction traces to a specific pattern rule — makes it auditable in a way that a neural model's predictions are not. Its limitation is equally clear: it captures only what is explicitly defined. The 54 unique entities the system found represent a subset of the full entity vocabulary in the corpus. Expanding the pattern list to include television viewing, recreational screen time, passive screen use, and other compound variants would increase recall substantially.

The sentence-level VADER sentiment analysis adds genuine analytical value on a real corpus. The negative sentence rate per health outcome entity is an insight that only emerges at scale — it is meaningless on 15 sentences and meaningful on 2,501. The finding that depression appears in negative framing 93% of the time while well-being appears in negative framing only 40% of the time reflects real differences in how clinical research frames those concepts, not statistical noise.

Ethical Assessment¶

Whose language, perspective, and context does the data represent, and who is missing? The five articles in this corpus are English-language peer-reviewed research, primarily from U.S. and European cohort studies. The populations studied are children and adolescents in structured research settings — not the children and families generating the informal discourse that parents, advocates, and platforms actually encounter. Research literature captures what scientists measure, not what families experience. A complete corpus would require news articles, parent forums, and practitioner guidance alongside the academic text.

What assumptions are being made about meaning, intent, or sentiment? VADER assigns sentiment to sentences based on a general-purpose lexicon. In research text, hedged language is common: "results suggest a modest negative association" scores differently than "screen time causes depression," even though both report a negative relationship. The VADER score reflects surface language affect, not the strength or certainty of the scientific claim. A sentence with a positive compound score may be reporting an intervention benefit — or it may simply be written in more optimistic framing. These are not the same thing, and the system cannot distinguish them.

How was the text data collected, and were consent and privacy appropriately considered? All five articles are published in peer-reviewed journals under Creative Commons open-access licenses. The BioC-PMC API is a publicly available resource maintained by NIH specifically for text mining research. No personal data, identifiable participant information, or proprietary content is included. The source articles themselves report on human subjects research that was conducted under IRB approval as documented in their methods sections.

How could the outputs be misused, misinterpreted, or over-trusted? The finding that screen time appears in negative-sentiment sentences 35% of the time and positive-sentiment sentences 37% of the time could be cited to argue that the research literature is ambivalent about screen time — that the evidence is balanced. That interpretation misreads the output. Sentiment score reflects the sentence's surface affect, not the direction of the causal evidence. The research literature shows consistent negative associations between excessive screen use and health outcomes. The sentiment distribution reflects how scientists write about those associations across different sentence types, not whether the associations themselves are mixed.

What responsibility does the data scientist have when the model produces harmful or misleading outcomes? The responsibility is documentation and framing. Every output from this system needs to be accompanied by a clear explanation of what the system measures and what it does not. Presenting entity counts as frequency of discussion is accurate. Presenting VADER sentiment scores as evidence of the scientific consensus direction is not accurate and would be misleading. When findings from this kind of system inform decisions — policy recommendations, platform regulation, clinical guidance — the data scientist must communicate the methodological limits as clearly as the findings themselves.
